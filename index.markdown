# Introduction

This is a digital edition of *The First Six Books of the Elements of Euclid, in which Coloured Diagrams and Symbols are Used Instead of Letters for the Greater Ease of Learners*, edited by Oliver Byrne and published in London by William Pickering in 1847. In this celebrated book, Byrne reworked Euclid's ancient geometry into a visual language of red, yellow, blue, and black figures, replacing the letters that traditionally keyed a diagram to its proof with the colored shapes themselves. The result is one of the most striking printed books of the nineteenth century, as much a landmark of graphic design as of mathematical pedagogy.

The edition presented here reproduces the full volume as a page-turnable facsimile, digitized from the copy held by the Thomas Fisher Rare Book Library at the University of Toronto. Readers can move through the book spread by spread, just as they would with the physical object, and consult the editorial sections below for context on the source, its maker, and the choices behind this edition.

Byrne's experiment was commercially unsuccessful in its own day, but it has drawn renewed attention for anticipating the visual logic of twentieth-century modernism. Our aim is to make the object itself easy to encounter and to situate it clearly for students, teachers, and the curious public.

# About this Source

Oliver Byrne (1810–1880) was an Irish civil engineer, mathematician, and prolific author who styled himself "Surveyor of Her Majesty's Settlements in the Falkland Islands." Across his career he produced textbooks on mathematics, engineering, and navigation, but he is remembered today almost entirely for this single, idiosyncratic book.[^2]

Byrne's premise was pedagogical. Euclid's *Elements*, compiled around 300 BCE, had been the backbone of geometric education in the West for more than two millennia. In the standard presentation, each diagram is labeled with letters, and the accompanying proof refers back to those letters: "the angle ABC is equal to the angle DEF," and so on. Byrne argued that this constant cross-referencing between text and figure taxed the learner's memory and slowed comprehension. His solution was to let color do the work of the letters. A triangle rendered in red, yellow, and blue could be named directly in the proof by showing the colored shapes themselves, so that the eye could follow the argument without translating symbols back into figures.[^3]

The book was printed by Charles Whittingham at the Chiswick Press and published by William Pickering, a partnership responsible for some of the finest English book production of the period. The color printing was demanding and expensive: each diagram required precise registration of multiple ink colors, and the typographic layout—with its careful balance of black letterpress and bright geometric figures—was unusually ambitious for a mathematical textbook. The expense is often cited as a reason the edition sold poorly and may have contributed to Pickering's later financial difficulties.[^4]

In its own century the book was something of a curiosity, admired by a few and largely ignored by the educational mainstream, which continued to rely on cheaper, letter-based editions. Its reputation was revived in the twentieth century by historians and theorists of design. The typographer Ruari McLean discussed it in his study of Victorian book design, and the information-design writer Edward Tufte has repeatedly held it up as an early and sophisticated example of visual explanation, in which graphics are not decoration but the primary carrier of meaning.[^5] Writers have noted that Byrne's palette and geometric abstraction seem to anticipate the visual vocabulary of the Bauhaus and De Stijl, movements that would not emerge for another seventy years. In 2010 the publisher Taschen issued a full facsimile, bringing the book to a wide modern audience.

For historians, the book sits at the intersection of several stories: the history of mathematical education and the long afterlife of Euclid; the economics and craft of color printing in the nineteenth century; and the prehistory of modern information design. It is a reminder that questions we think of as recent—how best to represent abstract relationships visually—have deep roots, and that a "failed" book can become, in retrospect, a pivotal one.

# About this Edition

This edition presents a digital facsimile of Byrne's 1847 *Elements* with the goal of making the complete object easy to read and navigate online.

- **Source artifact.** The facsimile reproduces the copy digitized from the Thomas Fisher Rare Book Library, University of Toronto, and made available through the Internet Archive under the identifier `firstsixbooksofe00eucl`. The volume may be found at [archive.org/details/firstsixbooksofe00eucl](https://archive.org/details/firstsixbooksofe00eucl). The original scan was produced by the University of Toronto scanning center at 300 ppi.
- **Presentation technology.** The page images are delivered as a PDF and rendered in the browser using [PDF.js](https://mozilla.github.io/pdf.js/), an open-source PDF viewer. The reader displays the book as a two-page spread on wider screens and as a single page on narrow screens, with controls for turning pages. All rendering happens in the reader's browser; no page images are pre-generated.
- **Emendations.** None. This edition presents the digitized pages as scanned, in their original sequence, without transcription, correction, or rearrangement. Any imperfections in the facsimile (such as scanning artifacts or tightly bound gutters) are those of the digitized source.
- **Limitations.** Because the PDF is rendered live in the browser, very large page counts may load slowly on older devices. Readers who prefer a different interface, or who need the underlying page images and OCR text, can consult the original at the Internet Archive link above.
